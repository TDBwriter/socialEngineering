# Source Excerpts and Context Checks

For each origin source: where it sits in the book, the line or idea the book leans on, and
whether the source actually says what the book uses it for. Chapter numbers follow the book
table of contents on the `book` branch.

**Status key**
- **Verified:** wording or finding checked this session against the text itself or against a
  source quoting it.
- **Recalled:** quoted from memory. The wording is believed accurate, but check it against print
  before publishing.
- **Paraphrase:** no direct quote given; the idea is summarized.

**Context flags** (⚑) mark places where the book uses a source differently from how its author
used it. Most are fine as "borrowing from," but say so on the page.

## Part One: Authority

### Chester I. Barnard, *The Functions of the Executive* (1938)
**Book:** Ch 2 (core belief), Ch 3, Ch 9
> "…the decision as to whether an order has authority or not lies with the persons to whom it is addressed, and does not reside in 'persons of authority' or those who issue these orders."

The four conditions for accepting an order: the person understands it, believes it fits the organization's purpose, believes it's compatible with their own interests, and is able to comply.
**Status:** Verified via secondary sources ([CMR](https://cmr.berkeley.edu/1970/11/13-1-authority-flow-theory-and-the-impact-of-chester-barnard/)). Check against the 1938 text on [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/functionsofexecu1938barn).

### Max Weber, *Economy and Society* (1922)
**Book:** Ch 2 (types of authority), Ch 14

⚑ Weber held that charisma couldn't be taught. That's the intended conflict with Antonakis.

## Preface and Afterword

### Immanuel Kant, *Perpetual Peace* (1795), Appendix II
**Book:** Preface, Afterword
> The negative formula: "All actions relating to the rights of other men are wrong, if the maxims from which they follow are inconsistent with publicity."

> **The affirmative formula:** "All maxims which require publicity, in order that they may not fail to attain their end, are in agreement both with right and politics."

The affirmative formula is the stronger line for this book. It is elicitation's success condition, a technique that works *because* it gets discovered, stated by Kant.
⚑ Kant applies the test to the weaker party too. His own example is a people revolting against a tyrant, and he rules that the revolt fails the test.
⚑ Kant wrote the test for "public right," meaning the conduct of states. Applying it to workplace conduct is an extension.
**Status:** Verified against the Mary Campbell Smith translation ([Gutenberg #50922](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/50922)).

### Karl R. Popper, *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945)
**Book:** Preface
> "The piecemeal engineer will… adopt the method of searching for, and fighting against, the greatest and most urgent evils of society, rather than searching for, and fighting for, its greatest ultimate good."

⚑ Popper's "social engineering" means public-policy reform, not interpersonal influence. That supports the claim that the term has a neutral origin, but the security meaning comes later.
**Status:** Recalled.
