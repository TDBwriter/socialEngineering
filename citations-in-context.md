# Citations in Context

A reading of every citation in [`outline.md`](outline.md): what the source actually
argues, how this outline applies it, and what that application is holding up elsewhere
in the fifty-two beats.

> **This file changes nothing.** No beat is renumbered, reordered, reworded or cut here.
> [`outline.md`](outline.md) remains the canonical spine and [`outline-v2.md`](outline-v2.md)
> remains the working draft. This is a third kind of document: scope and commentary, one
> entry per beat, written to be read beside the outline rather than instead of it.

Where [`bibliography.md`](bibliography.md) answers *why is this the origin*, this file
answers three different questions:

1. **What does the source actually say** — the specific mechanism or finding, located in
   the text, rather than the one-line summary the citation has collapsed into.
2. **How does this beat use it** — which of five relationships the beat has to its source,
   and what verb is honest on stage.
3. **What does the citation hold up elsewhere** — the other beats that depend on the same
   reading, so the outline reads as one argument rather than fifty-two separate claims.

Beat titles are quoted verbatim from `outline.md`.

---

## The five ways a citation is used here

The outline's own notes already split "origin of the concept" from "evidence for the claim."
That split has five positions, not two, and naming them fixes the verb that is honest on stage.

| Type | What it means | On stage |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct** | The beat asserts what the source asserts, in the source's own domain. | "X showed…" |
| **Transfer** | The source's mechanism is carried to a different population or setting. The mechanism is the evidence; the setting is yours. | "X found this in Y; the same mechanism runs here." |
| **Analogy** | The source supplies vocabulary and structure, not evidence. | "Borrowing from X…" |
| **Inversion** | The beat turns the source's purpose around — an attacker's catalogue read defensively, an obedience study read as a liberation result, a manager's tool aimed upward. | "X wrote this for the other side of the table." |
| **Foil** | The source is the position being corrected. | "X thought…; here is what happened when someone tested it." |

Roughly a third of the citations in this outline cannot support the sentence "research
shows." The beats where that matters most are flagged individually below.

## Act I: The Premise

### 1. Every technical system you've ever debugged is wrapped in a social system you never read the source for

**Trist & Bamforth (1951)** — *transfer* · *situation*

**The study.** In the Durham collieries, the hand-got method had organised miners into
small self-selected groups of two to six who worked the whole cycle, chose their own
partners, were paid as a group, and absorbed the anxiety of working underground through
mutual dependence. The longwall method mechanised the face and split that cycle across
three shifts of forty to fifty men doing single specialised tasks along a two-hundred-yard
face. Output did not rise as predicted. What appeared instead was inter-shift scapegoating,
competitive individualism, absenteeism and informal self-compensation. The conclusion was
not that the technology was bad but that the work system is a *joint* system: the technical
and social halves have to be optimised together, because changing one reorganises the other
whether you intend it or not.

**How the beat applies it.** Almost literally. This is the rare beat where the source is
already about a technical change failing through its social consequences, so the only
transfer is from mining to software. The beat's distinctive word is "source" — the social
system exists, it was designed by accident, and nobody wrote it down.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the licence for the whole of Act II. If the social
system is a system, then beats 6 through 11 are not metaphor-stacking but the same analysis
continued. It also plants *situation*: the longwall miners were not worse people after
mechanisation, they were the same people in a structure that produced scapegoating. That is
the premise beat 24 cashes in as the generous read, and the one beat 33 relies on when it
declines to treat a struggling manager as a bad person.

**Where it strains.** One case, one industry, one country, 1951, from a research programme
with an explicit intervention agenda. The beat generalises to "every technical system." The
defensible form is the weaker and still sufficient one: the social system is co-designed
whether or not anyone designs it. Modern companion already in the further reading:
DeMarco & Lister, *Peopleware* [1].

### 2. "Social engineering" is a neutral tool, and attackers borrowed it from salespeople, not the other way around

**Popper (1945)** — *direct* · *disclose*

**The source.** In *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, chapter 9, Popper contrasts
"utopian" or holistic social engineering, which designs society whole from a blueprint,
with "piecemeal" social engineering, which changes institutions incrementally and tests the
results. He endorses the second. The word is used approvingly, as the name for a
respectable activity, half a century before security adopted it for fraud.

**How the beat applies it.** As a licence: the term had a neutral philosophical life first,
so the beat's claim is a fact about the word's history rather than a rehabilitation attempt.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** More than the beat currently asks of it. Popper's
distinction is not just incrementalism — piecemeal engineering is *defined* by being open to
criticism and error correction, in public, by the people affected. That is the disclosure
test of beats 5 and 51 arriving two hundred pages early and in a different vocabulary. The
outline's ethical floor currently rests entirely on Kant; Popper gives it a second and more
operational footing, and the two citations are already three beats apart.

**Where it strains.** Popper did not coin the phrase — it circulated in Progressive-era
reform writing and in Dutch industrial welfare decades earlier. The bibliography is careful
to claim only "neutral use," which is correct and worth keeping. "Popper coined it" would be
wrong, and is the kind of thing a philosophy-literate audience member will raise.

**Cialdini (1984)** — *direct*

**The source.** The six principles came out of roughly three years of participant
observation inside compliance professions — car dealerships, fundraising rooms, advertising,
telemarketing — which Cialdini entered by training and working in them. The provenance is
commercial, not criminal.

**How the beat applies it.** This *is* the beat's claim. The order of borrowing is a fact
about where the research was done.

**Division of labour.** Popper supplies the word's respectable history; Cialdini supplies
the techniques' commercial provenance. Together they do the whole sentence, and neither
does it alone.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The second thing worth saying about Cialdini here is that
the book's stated purpose is self-defence — he opens by describing himself as a lifelong
patsy, and the chapters end with sections on how to refuse. That matters structurally: when
Act VI turns the six principles around at beats 35 and 36, it is not inverting Cialdini, it
is using him as written. The *influence* reading in Act III is the one that departs from the
source's framing. Worth knowing in a security room, where someone will have read the book.

### 3. Charisma is not a personality trait you were issued at birth; it is a build you spec into

**Weber (1922)** — *foil* · *granted* · *build*

**The source.** Weber's three pure types of legitimate authority are traditional,
charismatic and legal-rational. Charisma is "a certain quality of an individual personality
by virtue of which he is set apart from ordinary men," and Weber treats it as extraordinary
and unteachable. But he attaches a condition that is usually dropped from the summary: it
is *recognition by those subject to it* that makes charismatic authority valid. Charisma
nobody recognises is not weak charisma; in Weber's scheme it is not charisma at all.

**How the beat applies it.** As the old view, placed next to the experiment that tested it.
The outline's notes flag this as a "possible conflict," and it is — but a narrower one than
it looks.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Weber's recognition condition is the same structure as
Barnard's acceptance theory of authority at beats 7, 20 and 27, arriving sixteen years
earlier in a different literature. That is a real and useful finding for the core belief:
*granted* is not one author's idiosyncratic claim, it is independently the position of the
sociologist of authority and of the practising executive. It also narrows the beat-3
conflict, because Weber's charisma was never a property of the person alone — it was always
a relationship ratified by followers. "A build you spec into" contradicts Weber on
teachability. It agrees with him about where charisma lives.

**Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011)** — *direct* · *build*

**The study.** Two field experiments — MBA students, and middle managers in a Swiss firm —
training twelve charismatic leadership tactics: nine verbal (metaphors and analogies,
stories and anecdotes, contrasts, rhetorical questions, three-part lists, expressions of
moral conviction, reflecting the group's sentiments, setting high goals, conveying
confidence those goals can be met) and three nonverbal (animated voice, facial expressions,
gestures). Trained participants were rated significantly more charismatic and more
prototypically leader-like by observers.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and it is the only citation in Act I carrying
controlled evidence.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The twelve tactics are why the gaming metaphor is more than
a framing device: they are a discrete, enumerable, drillable list, which is what a skill
tree is. This is the empirical backing for beat 49's "speced" and beat 50's rehearsal, and
it is the single citation most responsible for the title being defensible.

**Where it strains.** The outcome measured is *observer ratings of charisma*, in short
interventions, not influence outcomes in an enterprise over quarters. The beat is safe; the
longer claim that this moves careers belongs to Deming at 48 and Casciaro & Lobo at 52, and
the outline should let those two carry it.

### 4. The enterprise is the hardest difficulty setting: high headcount, low bandwidth, no shared context

**Brooks (1975)** — *transfer* · *scarcity*

**The source.** Two chapters do the work. "The Mythical Man-Month" gives the arithmetic:
where every worker must coordinate with every other, intercommunication effort grows as
n(n−1)/2, which is why adding people to a late project makes it later. "Why Did the Tower of
Babel Fail?" answers its own question with communication, and then with organisation —
Brooks's point being that the Babel project had everything except the ability to talk to
itself.

**How the beat applies it.** The arithmetic transfers exactly; the "difficulty setting"
framing is the author's. The transfer is unusually safe, because Brooks's law depends on the
task being non-partitionable, and social work — building trust, establishing shared
context — is the most non-partitionable work there is. The claim is therefore *stronger* in
the beat's domain than in Brooks's.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is where *scarcity* enters, and it is the quantitative
floor under beat 13's attention argument and beat 12's throughput argument. It is also the
best available support for the uncited half of beat 14 — "where each one fails at scale" —
since n(n−1)/2 growth is precisely why dyadic persuasion primitives stop composing.

**Where it strains.** One of the five beats the notes identify as resting on a single
pre-1985 source, and the Babel half of the claim ("no shared context") is Brooks as
essayist rather than Brooks as arithmetician. The obvious modern companions — group-size
limits, and *Team Topologies*, already tagged at beat 8 — are not attached to this beat.

*The working draft adds three sources here.* **Peter & Hull (1969)** gives the name and
vocabulary for promotion past competence; **Benson, Li & Shue (2019)** gives its evidence;
**Robinson & Rousseau (1994)** gives the broken deal. Two notes. On the second: the
published study covers 214 firms, while the earlier working paper covered 131 firms, 38,843
sales workers and 1,553 promotions from 2005 to 2011. Both numbers are in circulation, so
"across 214 firms" should name the published version. On the third: the 54.8 per cent
figure is real, but it comes from 128 management-school alumni surveyed twice over two
years — a narrow sample for a claim about work in general, and the paper's title does more
rhetorical work than its N supports.

### 5. What this talk will and will not teach you (and the line I will not help you cross)

**Kant (1795), Appendix II** — *direct* · *disclose* · *build*

**The source.** Appendix II of *Toward Perpetual Peace* — "On the Harmony of Politics with
Morals According to the Transcendental Concept of Public Right" — gives the transcendental
formula of public right: all actions relating to the rights of others are unjust if their
maxim is not consistent with publicity. Kant is explicit that this is a *negative*
criterion. It detects injustice; it does not certify justice.

**How the beat applies it.** Exactly as Kant intended, as a one-way filter. "If it only
works when they don't know, don't do it" is the formula with the modality turned into
practice.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The bookend with beat 51, which the notes correctly
identify as the one piece of structure that does not survive translation to a book. The
negative-criterion point is the load-bearing precision: passing the publicity test does not
make an action good, it only fails to disqualify it. That caveat matters most at beat 51,
where the disclosure test is asked to license the balloon strategy — a much heavier lift
than disqualifying pretexting.

**The find.** The same appendix contains a second, *affirmative* formula, which the outline
does not cite: all maxims that require publicity in order not to fail of their end are in
accordance with both right and politics. That is a tighter fit for this project than the
negative formula, because the signature technique's success condition is being discovered —
the manager notices the pattern, sees that it works, adopts it. Elicitation does not merely
survive publicity; it *needs* publicity to achieve its aim, which is the affirmative
formula's exact antecedent. The ethical floor at beat 5 and the ethical claim at beat 51 are
currently resting on the weaker of the two formulas Kant supplies in the same appendix.

## Act II: Systems, Both Kinds

### 6. You already think in systems; you just stopped at the network boundary

**Wiener (1948)** — *analogy* · *situation*

**The source.** *Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine*
puts feedback, control and information into one framework covering servomechanisms and
organisms alike. The chapter on information, language and society extends it to human
groups — and this is the part worth knowing: Wiener extends it *cautiously*. He argues there
that the social sciences have short runs, poor statistics, and an observer who is inside the
system being observed, so cybernetic method has real limits in sociology.

**How the beat applies it.** As vocabulary and permission. The working draft already labels
it "an analogy, not evidence," which is right.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the hinge that lets Act II exist as an act, and it
opens *situation*: pointing a blameless-postmortem habit at people is Wiener's unified
control theory aimed at the org.

**The upgrade.** Wiener's own caution is a better disclaimer than the generic one. Naming it
on stage — the man who proposed the unified theory also wrote down why it under-delivers on
societies — pre-empts the exact objection a technical audience raises, and it costs one
sentence.

**Where it strains.** A mismatch worth knowing about: the beat's operative content in the
working draft is the blameless postmortem, which is Allspaw and the just-culture literature,
cited at beat 23. Wiener covers "you already think in systems." He does not cover the
postmortem. Beat 6's example currently lives on beat 23's citation.

### 7. Org charts are the documented API; the real call graph is informal and undocumented

**Barnard (1938)** — *direct* · *granted*

**The source.** Barnard, a working telephone-company president rather than an academic,
supplies two things this project cannot do without. Chapter 9 names the *informal
organisation* and argues it is not a defect in the formal one but a precondition for it:
formal organisation cannot function without the informal communication, cohesion and norms
that grow up around it. Chapter 12 gives the acceptance theory of authority — the decision
whether an order has authority lies with the person it is addressed to, not with the person
issuing it. Alongside it sit the *zone of indifference*, the band of orders people accept
without weighing them at all, and the "fiction of superior authority," Barnard's name for
the useful collective pretence that authority descends from above.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct on both halves. The org-chart-as-API framing is the
author's; the two-graph structure is Barnard's.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the most load-bearing citation in the project.
Beats 7, 20 and 27 are all Barnard, the core belief is Barnard, and the whole peer
leadership act is the acceptance theory applied laterally. If this reading were challenged,
more of the outline would move than for any other source.

**The upgrade.** Barnard's first named executive function is *to provide the system of
communication*. That gives the outline a much sharper account of what the practice actually
is: the recap at beats 9, 22, 28 and 38 is an executive function performed from below. Not a
workaround, not a shadow process — the specific function Barnard says executives exist to
supply, being supplied by someone else. That is why authority follows it, and it is a
cleaner mechanism than "reputation accrues." The zone of indifference is the second unused
piece: elicitation is what you do at the *boundary* of the zone, which is exactly where a
vague order puts you.

### 8. Conway's Law runs in both directions: your architecture is a confession about your relationships

**Conway (1968)** — *direct* in one reading, *analogy* in the other

**The source.** "How Do Committees Invent?" argues that organisations which design systems
are constrained to produce designs whose structure copies the organisation's communication
structure. Conway's own emphasis is on the *constraint*, and on its timing: the design
mirrors the organisation as it stood when the design was made, which is why he argues design
organisations must stay flexible.

**How the beat applies it.** The beat's two directions are not symmetrical in the source,
and the distinction is worth making because only one of them needs a second citation. The
*diagnostic* direction — read the architecture to learn the relationships — is a
contrapositive of Conway's homomorphism and is safe from Conway alone; "a confession about
your relationships" is exactly this reading and is well founded. The *interventionist*
direction — restructure the teams to get the architecture you want — is the inverse Conway
manoeuvre, which is Thoughtworks and *Team Topologies*, already tagged at this beat in the
further reading. Conway does not prescribe it.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the beat that makes the social system *observable*,
which the talk needs because its audience trusts artifacts more than impressions. The
working draft's addition — a manager who cannot define "done" shows up as unclear interfaces
and rework — is the diagnostic reading applied to beat 37's ambiguity, and it is the
tightest link between Act II and Act VI.

### 9. Information in an enterprise obeys routing rules, and most of it is dropped at the first hop

**Bartlett (1932)** — *transfer* · *record*

**The source.** *Remembering* reports two paradigms: repeated reproduction, where one
subject recalls the same material over time, and serial reproduction, where each subject
passes material to the next. The material was most famously "The War of the Ghosts," a
folk tale chosen for being culturally unfamiliar to Cambridge subjects. Bartlett's finding is
the one that matters here, and it is not merely that detail is lost. It is that recall is
*reconstructive*: material is assimilated to the subject's existing schema — shortened,
rationalised and conventionalised toward what the receiver already expects. The distortion
has a direction.

**How the beat applies it.** As the routing-and-loss claim, which is the weaker half of
Bartlett's finding.

**The upgrade.** The directional half is far more useful to this outline. A manager's
instruction does not just arrive degraded; it arrives *reshaped toward whatever the receiver
already believes the project is*. That changes the job of the recap at beats 28 and 38 from
"a durable copy" to "a schema check" — the recap earns its keep by exposing the direction in
which you bent the request, which neither party can see from inside their own schema. It
also predicts which requests get misrouted worst: the ones that are culturally unfamiliar to
the receiver, which is the business-to-engineering direction and back.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Beat 38 is the same citation doing the defensive job, and
the pair is one of the outline's cleanest *polarity* examples: one set of experiments
explains both why you need the record and why the record is not an accusation. Loftus [38]
carries the modern malleability evidence.

### 10. Trust is the latency budget of an organization

**Arrow (1972)** — *transfer* · *scarcity*

**The source.** "Gifts and Exchanges," written in the context of Titmuss's work on blood
donation, argues that virtually every commercial transaction contains an element of trust;
that trust is an enormously efficient lubricant of a social system, saving a great deal of
trouble in verification and haggling; and — the claim the outline needs most — that trust is
*not a commodity that can be bought*. It behaves like an externality, closer to a public
good than to a purchasable input.

**How the beat applies it.** The lubricant claim transfers to the latency metaphor directly.
Where trust is high a one-line request executes; where it is low every request needs a spec,
a recap and a check-in, and that overhead is Arrow's verification cost made visible.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The unbuyability claim is what keeps the practice from being
circular. You cannot ask for trust, so the only available move is to lower the cost of
verifying you — which is what the recap does. It is also the economic half of beat 43: when
the artifact becomes free, what stays scarce is the thing that was never purchasable.

### 11. Every org has a hidden dependency graph, and reputation is its service registry

**Granovetter (1973)** — *transfer*, partly · *lateral*

**The source.** "The Strength of Weak Ties" argues that weak ties, not strong ones, are the
bridges between otherwise disconnected clusters, and therefore the paths along which novel
information travels. The empirical backing is his job-search work: people heard about the
jobs they took more often through acquaintances than through close friends. The structural
point is the forbidden triad — a bridge must be a weak tie, because strong ties close into
cliques.

**How the beat applies it.** Granovetter supports *reach*: information about you travels
further through acquaintances than through close collaborators. He does not supply
reputation-as-registry. That framing is the author's, and the nearest citation for it is
Burt's structural holes, already tagged at this beat in the further reading.

**The strongest form.** Weak ties are how your reputation gets *queried*. The people who
decide about you mostly do not know you; they know someone who does, and the lookup runs
across a bridge. That is faithful to Granovetter, and a better fit for "service registry"
than the reach claim alone.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the structural reason *lateral* is not a consolation
prize. It also underwrites the working draft's answer to the hardest Q&A question at beat
32 — if you leave, or a reorg decides for you, the relationships and the reputation go with
you — because weak ties are precisely the ties that survive a change of team.

## Act III: The Mechanics of Influence

### 12. Influence is throughput, not volume: being heard beats being loud

**Shannon (1948)** — *analogy* · *scarcity*

**The source.** "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" gives entropy, channel capacity,
the noisy-channel coding theorem, and redundancy as the remedy for noise. It also contains
the sentence that governs every use of Shannon in this outline: the semantic aspects of
communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem. Shannon's theory is about reliably
transmitting a *selected* message. It says nothing about whether the message is any good.

**How the beat applies it.** As vocabulary, and more precisely than the beat currently
claims. The Shannon-faithful mapping is: loudness is amplitude, not rate; throughput is
bounded by capacity and degraded by noise; and the remedy for noise is better encoding and
redundancy, never more amplitude. "Being heard beats being loud" is therefore not a
sentiment — it is what the model predicts. You cannot shout past a capacity limit. You
encode for the channel.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Paired with Simon at 13, this is the engineering case for
the whole delivery style of the practice: one bundled ask, one piece of evidence, a short
recap. Beat 28 is the same citation doing the compression job.

**Where it strains.** The semantic disclaimer is the honest limit, and it is the right thing
to say out loud in an engineering room: Shannon can tell you why a long rambling ask fails,
and nothing about why a clear one persuades. That second half is Aristotle's department at
19 and 30. The notes also flag the bibliographic detail — the paper ran in two parts, July
and October 1948.

### 13. Attention is the scarcest resource in the building, and you are competing with 400 unread emails

**Simon (1971)** — *direct* · *scarcity*

**The source.** The line everyone quotes: what information consumes is the attention of its
recipients, so a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. The half that is
almost always dropped is Simon's design conclusion. He argues that an information-processing
system earns its keep by *absorbing* demands on attention rather than generating more
information — that the useful system reduces what reaches a person.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and it is the cleanest *direct* in Act III.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The dropped half is the more valuable one and it reaches
three other beats. It is the reason the recap is short rather than complete (beat 28), the
reason the questions arrive bundled rather than one at a time (beat 27), and — this is the
sharp one — it reframes the entire AI act. A tool that produces more polished artifacts
faster is the *opposite* of what Simon prescribed. By his own design criterion, generative AI
is an attention-consuming technology dressed as an attention-saving one. That is a precise,
citable reason why beat 43 follows from beat 42 rather than merely coming after it, and it
is available from a citation the outline already has at both beats.

### 14. Reciprocity, consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity: the six primitives, and where each one fails at scale

**Cialdini (1984)** — *direct* on the first half · *polarity*

**The source.** The six principles are presented as heuristics that normally serve us well
and can therefore be exploited — Cialdini's "click, whirr" framing, borrowed from
fixed-action patterns in ethology. The principles are not tricks; they are shortcuts with
legitimate uses, which is why the same chapter can teach both the use and the refusal. A
seventh principle, Unity, arrives later — introduced in *Pre-Suasion* (2016) and folded into
the expanded *Influence* (2021).

**How the beat applies it.** Direct on the enumeration. The working draft's addition is the
important one and it is faithful to the source: each principle has an honest form and a
hidden one — real scarcity against a manufactured deadline, real authority against an
impersonated one — so the disclosure test sorts *within* each principle rather than between
them. That is the structural move that lets Act III and Act VI use one citation without
contradiction.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the outline's *polarity* pivot. The six principles
appear as tools at 14, as the attack recipe at 35, as the shared threat model at 36, and as
what AI amplifies at 46. One reading of one book is carrying four beats across three acts.

**Where it strains.** The second half of the beat's own title — "where each one fails at
scale" — has no citation behind it, and Cialdini cannot supply one. His principles are
dyadic and consumer-facing; scale failure is not a question his fieldwork asked. The
available support is already in the outline: Brooks at 4 for why coordination cost explodes,
Simon at 13 for why attention does not scale with headcount. As it stands this is the
outline's largest uncited claim, and it sits in a beat title.

### 15. Framing determines the answer more reliably than evidence does

**Tversky & Kahneman (1981)** — *direct* on the mechanism, *overstated* in the beat's wording

**The source.** The Asian disease problem. Given a gain frame — 200 people will be saved
against a one-third chance of saving 600 — a large majority took the certain option. Given
the logically identical loss frame — 400 will die against a two-thirds chance that 600 will
die — the majority reversed and took the gamble. The finding is a failure of *invariance*:
description changes choice while the facts are held constant.

**How the beat applies it.** The beat claims framing beats evidence. The experiment shows
something different and arguably more striking: framing changes the answer *even when the
evidence does not change*. Tversky and Kahneman never ran framing against evidence in a
contest, so the comparative is not theirs. The weaker sentence is both defensible and more
quotable.

**Where it strains.** This is the beat the notes already call the most exposed, and the
exposure is worse than a missing modern companion. The working draft's content for beat 15 —
"so I don't waste your time" versus "you never told me" — is not gain/loss framing at all.
It is face-work, which is Goffman at 18. The citation and the example have drifted apart.

**The repair available without changing the beat.** The T&K-faithful applications are
already in the outline at other numbers: beat 30, translating work into the manager's units,
and beat 40, the tradeoff question, which presents a fixed constraint as a choice among
gains rather than as an accusation of failure. If beat 15 keeps this citation, those are the
examples that match it, and the face-work example belongs to its own source.

### 16. Specificity is charisma: vague asks get vague outcomes

**Locke (1968)** — *direct* · *elicit*

**The source.** Specific, hard goals produce higher performance than vague exhortations like
"do your best," and goals mediate the effect of incentives — people's conscious intentions
are what regulate their effort. Three conditions in the later theory matter for this
outline: the goal must be *accepted*, there must be *feedback* against it, and the effect is
moderated by *task complexity*.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and this is the best-grounded beat in the outline. It
is also the engine of the whole practice: if specific goals outperform vague ones, then the
questions that convert a vague expectation into a specific one are doing measurable work
rather than merely feeling tidier.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The acceptance condition links straight back to Barnard at
7 and 27 — a goal you have not accepted does not produce the effect, which is the same
structure as an order that has not been accepted carrying no authority. The feedback
condition is why the check-in rhythm at beats 29 and 32 is not optional garnish: without
feedback, Locke's effect does not appear, so a practice with goals but no check-ins is not
the practice the citation supports.

**The answer it supplies to the hardest Q&A question.** Someone will ask what to do when
nobody *can* say what done looks like, because the work is genuinely novel. The task-complexity
moderator is the answer, and it comes from the same literature: for complex, novel tasks a
specific outcome goal helps less, and a *learning* goal — what we need to find out, by when,
and how we will know we found it — outperforms it. That distinguishes the vagueness
elicitation should dissolve from the vagueness it should convert, and it pairs exactly with
the Eisenberg criterion at beat 37.

### 17. The highest-leverage sentence in any meeting is "Let me make sure I understand what you need"

**Rogers & Roethlisberger (1952)** — *direct* · *elicit*

**The source.** Two essays printed together. Rogers argues the major barrier to
communication is our tendency to evaluate, and proposes a rule: each person may speak for
themselves only after restating the previous speaker's ideas and feelings accurately *and
to that speaker's satisfaction*. He also notes the cost — the rule takes courage, because
genuinely understanding another position risks being changed by it. Roethlisberger's half
is organisational: the two levels on which a supervisor and a worker talk past each other,
and why the hierarchy makes it worse.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, almost to the word.

**The precision that matters.** "To that speaker's satisfaction." You do not get to decide
you understood. That makes the restatement an acceptance test, and it is the same structure
as Barnard's acceptance theory one act later — authority is granted by the receiver,
comprehension is certified by the speaker. The outline has these two in different acts
without noting that they are the same move pointed in opposite directions, and saying so
would tie Act III to Act V with one sentence.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the opening move of every elicitation conversation and
therefore the operational root of beats 27, 31, 35, 40 and 47. Huang et al. [17] supplies
the modern finding that question-asking increases liking, which is the beat's charisma claim.

### 18. Status and status-signaling are real variables, and ignoring them does not zero them out

**Weber (1922)** — *direct*

**The source.** In "Class, Status, Party," Weber separates status — social honour, expressed
through a style of life and defended by status groups — from class, which is economic, and
from party, which is organised power. The point for this beat is that status is a
*distinct* variable with its own dynamics, not an epiphenomenon of pay grade.

**Weber, second use.** The working draft's Act VI preamble leans on a third Weberian
concept, legal-rational authority: a manager's power comes from the organisation's rules, so
those rules also bound it, and punishing someone for asking what done looks like would
require documentation that shows a reasonable question being punished. That is a good
argument and it is currently uncited. It is Weber, and it is the same book.

**Goffman (1959)** — *direct* · *disclose*

**The source.** *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* treats social life
dramaturgically: performances, front and back regions, audience segregation, idealisation,
and — crucially for this outline — *teams*, the sets of people who cooperate to sustain a
single definition of the situation. The companion paper the working draft adds, "On
Face-Work" (1955), supplies the mechanism: face is a mutual construct, people are obliged to
protect each other's face as well as their own, and there is a named practice for ignoring a
flaw in someone's performance so the performance can continue — *studied nonobservance*.

**How the beat applies it.** Goffman answers the beat's "does not zero them out": you cannot
abstain from a performance you are already in, because your abstention is also read.

**The term the working draft is reaching for.** The "ego escape" in the draft — the gap
becomes something you are working out together rather than something they missed — is
studied nonobservance exactly. Naming it costs nothing and buys a lot, because it makes the
move a documented social convention rather than a private kindness the speaker invented.

**What it holds up elsewhere, and this is the big one.** Face-work is the outline's best
example of its own central claim. Everyone involved knows face-work is happening; it is
conventional, mutually understood, and it still works. That is the existence proof for beat
2's split between techniques that survive disclosure and techniques that do not, and it is
the strongest single support for the ethical floor at 51 — because the usual objection to
the floor is that *all* influence stops working once named, and face-work is the
counterexample sociology has had on the shelf since 1955.

### 19. Narrative beats data in the room; data beats narrative in the follow-up

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book I.2** — *analogy*, and the ranking is not Aristotle's

**The source.** I.2 gives the three artistic proofs — ethos, pathos, logos — and says the
speaker's character is close to the most effective means of persuasion. It also calls the
enthymeme the body of proof. Aristotle does not rank narrative above argument; if anything
he privileges the reasoned proof and treats the three as suited to different occasions and
audiences.

**How the beat applies it.** The citation licenses the *existence* of non-logical proof, not
the beat's ordering claim. On stage, "Aristotle ranked story over data" would be wrong and
checkable.

**The faithful support, which Aristotle does supply.** Book II.1 contains the claim the beat
actually needs: our judgments when we are pleased and friendly are not the same as when we
are pained and hostile. That is a statement about the *moment of judgment*, which is exactly
"in the room." Pairing I.2 for the three proofs with II.1 for the timing gives the beat its
own citation for both halves, from a book the outline already cites at three other beats.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the delivery rule for *record*: the story carries the
room, the recap carries the facts, and the split is why the recap can be unsentimental
without being cold. The Heaths [19] and Small, Loewenstein & Slovic [19] carry the modern
evidence for narrative's advantage in the moment.

## Act IV: Peer Leadership Without Authority

### 20. Most of your real leverage is lateral, and none of it comes with a title

**Barnard (1938)** — *transfer* · *granted* · *lateral*

**How the beat applies it.** The acceptance theory, pointed sideways. Barnard's subject is
the vertical case — why subordinates accept orders — and the beat extends it to peers, where
there is no order to accept in the first place. That extension is sound in Barnard's own
terms, because his claim is that authority is *constituted* by acceptance; if so, acceptance
without a title is not a lesser kind of authority but the only kind there ever was.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This beat and beat 27 are the two faces of the same citation,
and together they are the reason the talk is not two unrelated halves. The SRE model in the
working draft is the clean illustration: no authority beyond facts and reasoning, and
results anyway.

*The working draft adds the evidence Barnard cannot give.* **Kipnis, Schmidt & Wilkinson
(1980)** catalogued the influence tactics people actually use inside organisations and found
rational persuasion the most common upward. **Kipnis & Schmidt (1988)** clustered managers
into four upward-influence styles and compared outcomes: *tacticians*, who lead with
reasoning, received the highest performance ratings; *shotguns*, who use everything and lean
on assertiveness, received the lowest, earned less, and reported more job tension and
personal stress; *ingratiators* landed in the middle; *bystanders* rarely attempted
influence at all. This is the best empirical support in the project for its central
practical claim, and it is stronger than the beat currently says: leading with reason is not
merely more ethical, it measurably outperforms the alternatives on the outcomes people care
about.

**One finding to handle honestly on stage.** The same 1988 paper reports that gender
moderated the relationship between upward-influence style and supervisors' evaluations —
female ingratiators were rated more favourably than their male counterparts. A talk telling
a mixed audience that reason-based influence pays should know that the measured payoff was
not uniform across the room. This is the same concern the notes raise about the composition
of the origin list, arriving as an empirical result rather than a bibliographic one, and
it is a better answer to that Q&A than the bibliography alone can give.

### 21. Credit is a renewable resource, and spending it on others compounds your own

**Gouldner (1960)** — *direct* · *lateral*

**The source.** The norm of reciprocity makes two minimal demands: help those who have
helped you, and do not injure those who have helped you. Gouldner's contribution beyond
naming it is functional. Reciprocity is a *starting mechanism* — it gets exchange going
before any institutional role obligations exist — and it works because what is owed stays
*indeterminate*. The debt is real and unspecified, which is what keeps the relationship
open.

**How the beat applies it.** The indeterminacy claim is the beat's compounding mechanism,
and it is worth stating explicitly, because it comes with an instruction. An unspecified
obligation generates further exchange; the moment you specify it — name the favour, ask for
the return — you convert it into a settled account and the compounding stops. "Credit is
renewable" is true for exactly as long as you decline to invoice.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Gouldner's *second* demand does unacknowledged structural
work later. The working draft argues at beat 36 that an ego-driven manager stops targeting
you once you become the source of their standing, and grounds that in the attacker's
incentives. Gouldner grounds it in a norm: injuring someone who has helped you is the one
move the reciprocity norm forbids most strongly. Two independent reasons for the same
prediction is a better footing for the balloon strategy at 33 than either alone.

**Where it strains.** Gouldner describes a norm, which means it describes what people are
expected to do and not what every person does. The counter-case is the taker who absorbs
credit without reciprocating, and the working draft already has the right citation for it:
Grant's distinction between selfless and "otherish" giving, with the written record and the
end date as the self-protective half.

### 22. Being the person who makes other people's work legible is a full-time superpower

**Scott (1998)** — *inversion*

**The source.** *Seeing Like a State* is a critique. Legibility is Scott's term for the
simplifications a state imposes so it can count, tax, conscript and plan — cadastral maps,
permanent surnames, scientific forestry, standardised measures — and his thesis is that
these schemes fail, sometimes catastrophically, because the simplification discards *metis*,
the local practical knowledge that made the original system work. Legibility in Scott is
something done *to* people by a distant authority that does not have to live with the
consequences.

**How the beat applies it.** With the valence reversed. That is a legitimate borrowing and
the beat should own it on stage, because a reader who knows the book will flinch otherwise.
The defensible version: Scott's objection is to legibility imposed from outside and above by
an authority that then overrides local knowledge. What the beat describes is a practitioner
making their own group's work legible voluntarily, from inside, while retaining the metis.
That is not the thing Scott attacks.

**The cost Scott predicts, which the outline has not yet priced in.** Anything you make
legible becomes governable by people who do not have your metis. That is the structural risk
of the recap practice, and it bears directly on beat 38's claim that the record is a
parachute: the same document that protects you also hands a distant party a simplified model
of your work, and simplified models become targets. Scott is the citation that predicts this,
and the outline currently cites him only for the upside. Naming the risk and the mitigation —
the recap records decisions and dates rather than metrics that can be optimised against — is
a stronger position than hoping nobody asks.

**Reilly, "Being Glue" (2019).** The working draft adds it for the invisibility cost, which
is right, with one tension worth tracking: Reilly's own advice is partly to *stop* absorbing
glue work that is blocking recognition, which sits awkwardly beside "full-time superpower."
The resolution in her own terms is that glue work needs to be made visible and counted, not
abandoned — which is the same argument as making the recap legible, pointed at your own work
instead of your manager's.

### 23. Disagreement is a protocol, not an event: how to dissent and keep the connection open

**Rogers & Roethlisberger (1952)** — *direct* · *elicit*

The restatement rule supplies the protocol, and Rogers's note about courage supplies the
reason it needs to be a protocol rather than a disposition: he observed that the rule is
hard precisely when the disagreement is real.

**Asch (1951)** — *transfer*

**The study.** Seven to nine people judge which of three lines matches a standard. All but
one are confederates giving a unanimous wrong answer. Participants conformed on about a
third of critical trials, and around three-quarters conformed at least once. Group size
effects plateau at three or four confederates.

**How the beat applies it.** Asch is not here as a claim about meetings. He is here to
establish the *cost* of being the only person who says the uncomfortable thing — which is
why the beat has to be about a protocol you can follow under social pressure rather than an
act of character you perform when you feel brave.

**Division of labour.** Rogers gives you what to say; Asch tells you what it costs to say
it; Allspaw's just-culture post, added in the working draft, gives the institutional form
that makes it cheap.

**The asymmetry worth naming.** A blameless postmortem works partly because the institution
has sanctioned blamelessness in advance — the rules of the room are published. Disagreeing
with your manager has no such sanction, and that gap is exactly what Asch measures. The
working draft's move at beat 25, making the question a team norm, is what manufactures the
missing sanction, which makes 23 and 25 one argument rather than two.

### 24. The "generous read" is a debugging technique for human behavior

**Heider (1958)** — *direct* · *situation*

**The source.** *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* builds a systematic account of
naive psychology — how ordinary people explain behaviour — and locates the central asymmetry:
behaviour is attributed to the person far more readily than to the situation, because, in
Heider's phrase, behaviour engulfs the field. The *name* "fundamental attribution error" is
Ross (1977), which the bibliography already records correctly.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and the engulfing-the-field formulation is the useful
one for an engineering audience: the person is perceptually foreground and the conditions
are background, which is a sampling problem, not a moral failing. That frames the generous
read as a debugging technique — look at the conditions you cannot see from here — rather
than as an exercise in charity, which is what the beat's own wording promises.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the beat that makes *situation* operational, and the
practice depends on it in both directions. Pointed outward it produces the refusal to
diagnose, which the working draft carries to beats 33 and 36. Pointed inward it produces the
admission at beat 31 that you probably cannot see what your manager is measured on.

**An unused piece.** Heider's other contribution, balance theory — people adjust their
liking and their beliefs to keep their relationships consistent — is a direct account of why
the water-cooler conversation at beat 25 either hardens a coalition against the manager or
turns it around, with few stable positions in between.

*The working draft's additions are handled unusually well here.* Kruger & Dunning (1999) is
introduced as a framework rather than a diagnosis, and Nuhfer et al. (2016) is cited for the
measurement critique — random data reproduce the famous chart, and the "Mount Stupid" curve
is not in the original paper. Stating the dispute before an audience member does is the
right call, and it is also the beat's own thesis applied to the beat: the effect describes a
condition, not a defect in a person.

### 25. Build the coalition before the meeting, because the meeting is a ratification ceremony

**Asch (1951)** — *transfer* · *lateral*

**The finding that matters here.** In the variation with a single dissenting partner,
conformity collapses — errors fall from roughly a third of critical trials to around five per
cent. The crucial detail, and the one that changes the beat's practical advice: what breaks
the pressure is the loss of *unanimity*, not the arrival of a correct ally. A partner who
dissents in a different wrong direction also sharply reduces conformity.

**Why that matters.** It means the coalition you build before the meeting does not need to
agree with you. It needs to be willing to speak. "Get one person to say something" is a far
lower bar than "get one person onto your side," it is more honest, and it is what the
experiment actually supports.

**Where the beat overreaches.** Asch's ally effect is about the *dissenter's capacity to
resist*, not about a coalition's ability to flip a room. "The meeting is a ratification
ceremony" is a claim about organisational decision-making, and the citation for it is Kotter
[25], already in the further reading.

**Milgram (1974)** — *inversion*

The working draft cites the two-peers-rebel variation, in which two fellow teachers refuse
to continue and full obedience falls to about ten per cent. That is accurate, and it is the
best use of Milgram anywhere in the outline: an experiment conducted to measure how far
people will go under authority, read instead as a measurement of how little it takes to stop
them. Pairing it with Asch gives the beat two independent demonstrations that one visible
refusal changes what authority can do.

**Miller & Ross (1975)** — *direct*, and carefully cited

The working draft's own note is correct and worth preserving: this review found support for
self-enhancing attributions of *success* and weak support for self-protective attributions of
failure, so the citation is used only for the half it supports. The strategy at 25 and 33
needs exactly that half — the manager will read the team's success as their own leadership —
and does not need the other.

### 26. Mentorship is distributed caching: you are warming someone else's context

**Kram (1985)** — *direct* · *lateral*

**The source.** Kram separates mentoring's *career* functions — sponsorship, exposure and
visibility, coaching, protection, challenging assignments — from its *psychosocial*
functions — role modelling, acceptance and confirmation, counselling, friendship. She also
describes the relationship's four phases: initiation, cultivation, separation, redefinition.

**How the beat applies it.** The coaching and psychosocial functions, transferred to a peer
and then, in the working draft, pointed upward at the manager. That inversion is the beat's
real content and it is the riskiest thing in Act IV to say out loud, which is presumably why
the draft says "quietly."

**What it holds up elsewhere.** The four phases are the unused piece, and they matter more
than the beat suggests. Kram's claim is that *separation and redefinition are normal and
necessary*, not symptoms of failure — a mentoring relationship that never ends has not
succeeded, it has stalled. That gives the end date at beat 32 a grounded precedent instead
of an arbitrary choice, and it supplies the answer to the obvious objection that setting an
end date on helping someone is cold. It is not cold; it is the phase structure the
literature describes, applied on purpose.

## Act V: Navigating Managerial Relationships

### 27. Managing up is not flattery; it is reducing your manager's uncertainty

**Barnard (1938)** — *direct* · *granted* · *elicit*

**The source.** Barnard names four conditions that must *all* hold before a person treats a
communication as authoritative: they can and do understand it; they believe it is not
inconsistent with the organisation's purpose; they believe it is consistent with their own
interest as a whole; and they are mentally and physically able to comply.

**How the beat applies it.** The working draft maps the four conditions onto the question
set, and the mapping is tight enough to be the act's spine: "What does done look like?"
tests understanding; "What's driving this?" tests organisational purpose; "If I can only do
two of the three, which two?" tests ability. The third condition, personal interest, is the
one the questions do not test — and it is the one beat 30 covers by aiming the work at what
makes the manager look good upward.

**The structural payoff.** A vague order fails Barnard's first condition before anyone has
done anything wrong. That is the whole act in one sentence, and it is the reason the practice
is not insubordination: the questions are not a challenge to authority, they are the
*precondition* for authority existing at all. On stage, the old line — when I say jump, you
say how high — makes the point for free, as the working draft notes: "how high" is already a
clarifying question, and the rest of the question set is the same move continued.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** With beats 7 and 20, this completes Barnard's three-beat
arc: the informal graph exists, acceptance constitutes authority, and therefore the
questions that produce acceptance are where influence comes from. The zone of indifference
is the frame for the whole act — orders inside the zone get no questions, and elicitation is
what happens at its boundary.

### 28. Your manager is a lossy compression layer between you and the org, so control what gets compressed

**Shannon (1948)** — *analogy* · *record*

The faithful mapping is rate–distortion: when a channel cannot carry everything, *something*
is discarded, and the only way to influence which something is to choose the encoding
upstream. The recap is the pre-encoded message. "Control what gets compressed" is a precise
restatement of that, and it is the strongest Shannon use in the outline because it depends
on the part of the theory that is actually about loss rather than about meaning.

**Goffman (1959)** — *direct* · *record*

Goffman is doing the heavier lift at this beat, though he is listed second. The useful
concept is the *team*: with respect to their own boss, you and your manager are a
performance team sustaining one definition of the situation. That is what makes the recap a
gift rather than a dossier — it hands your manager clean language that will hold up on their
front stage, in a region you are not in and cannot manage yourself.

**Division of labour.** Shannon explains why something must be dropped; Goffman explains why
your manager will accept your help choosing. Neither answers the other's question, and the
beat needs both.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the hinge between *record* as a courtesy in Act V
and *record* as a shield in Act VI. The same artifact does both jobs, and the fact that it
is genuinely useful to the manager is what keeps beat 38 from reading as file-building. Evans
on brag documents [28] and Fournier [28] carry the practitioner versions.

### 29. Bad news early is a gift; bad news late is a liability transfer

**Rosen & Tesser (1970)** — *direct* · *elicit*

**The study.** Subjects were markedly less willing to pass on bad news than good news, and
the reluctance tracked their own discomfort at being associated with the message rather than
any judgment about whether the recipient needed it. They named it the MUM effect — keeping
mum about undesirable messages.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and the mechanism is what gives the beat its moral
force. The reluctance is about *your* discomfort, not their interest, which means "bad news
late" is a transfer of discomfort now into a transfer of liability later. Naming whose
feelings are actually in play is more persuasive than an appeal to integrity, and it is what
the study found.

**The extension the outline has not used.** The MUM effect is not a property of
subordinates. Your manager is subject to it too, and the specific bad news a struggling
manager is most likely to withhold is *that there is no plan*. That is a causal account of
where vague expectations come from, built from a citation the outline already has, and it
links beat 29 forward to beat 37's ambiguity and beat 31's invisible objectives. It also
reinforces the refusal to diagnose at beat 24: silence that looks like evasion may be the
same effect the talk has just asked the audience to forgive in themselves.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the reason the check-in rhythm is load-bearing rather
than procedural: a cadence makes bad news routine, which is the only reliable defence
against an effect driven by the discomfort of volunteering it. The Columbia accident report
[29] is the catastrophic case already in the further reading.

### 30. Translate your work into their units: risk, cost, headcount, and time-to-market

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book II** — *direct*

**The source.** Book II is where the *Rhetoric* turns to the audience: the emotions and
how they are aroused and allayed, and then the character types of audiences — the young, the
old, those in their prime, the wealthy, the powerful, the well-born — each with
characteristic ends and susceptibilities. The method is audience analysis, and the premise
is that a proof is addressed to someone in particular.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct. "Their units" is Aristotle's doctrine stated in an
engineer's vocabulary: argue from the premises your audience already holds, toward the ends
they already have.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** It is the one beat in Act V that works on a manager whose
motives you do not respect, which is why the working draft leans on it for the ego-driven
case: clarity that makes them look good upward is the thing they reliably want, so the
translation does not require shared values. That makes 30 the bridge between the act's
general practice and beat 36's "one strategy for every manager." Conger [30] and
*Accelerate* [30] supply the modern unit list.

### 31. Know what your manager is being measured on, or you are optimizing a function you can't see

**Kerr (1975)** — *direct* · *situation* · *elicit*

**The source.** Kerr catalogues reward systems that pay for A while hoping for B — in
medicine, universities, the military, government and business — and names four causes:
fascination with objective, measurable criteria; overemphasis on highly visible behaviours;
hypocrisy; and a preference for morality or equity over efficiency.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct transfer. The beat's contribution is to point the
analysis at a specific person — the manager's reward function is the one you cannot see, and
not seeing it means you are optimising blind.

**The link the outline is missing.** Kerr's second cause, overemphasis on highly visible
behaviours, is the *same bug* beat 33 proposes to run forward. Kerr says systems reward what
is visible; Benson, Li and Shue measured a promotion system doing exactly that and paying
for it in managerial quality. The two halves of the balloon argument are a 1975 diagnosis
and a 2019 measurement of the same mechanism, and Kerr is currently cited only in Act V
while the mechanism is used in Act V and Act IV's successor beat. Connecting them costs one
sentence and turns the balloon from a provocation into the predicted output of a documented
failure mode.

**Where it strains.** Another of the five single-source pre-1985 beats. Kerr's essay is
argument plus examples, not measurement — which is fine, since the measurement arrived in
2019 and the outline already owns it elsewhere.

### 32. Skip-levels, sponsors, and the difference between someone who likes you and someone who will spend capital on you

**Kram (1985)** — *direct* · *lateral*

**The source and the fit.** This is the most exact citation in the outline. Kram's taxonomy
puts *sponsorship* among the career functions and *acceptance-and-confirmation* and
*friendship* among the psychosocial ones. The beat's distinction between someone who likes
you and someone who will spend capital on you is not an analogy or an extension — it is
Kram's own category boundary, in her own terms. Hewlett [32] is the modern popularisation.

**Hirschman (1970)** — *direct*, with one attribution to fix

**The source.** *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* sets out two responses to decline in a firm,
organisation or state: leave, or speak up. Loyalty is the third term, and Hirschman's role
for it is specific — loyalty raises the cost of exit and thereby makes voice more likely.
He also argues that an easy exit option *atrophies* voice: where leaving is cheap, nobody
bothers to fix anything.

**How the beat applies it.** As the map of the underling's real options, and the application
is better than the draft claims for itself. The working draft's close — quitting stops
looking like an option at all, because by now this is your team — is not a sentimental flourish
sitting beside Hirschman. It *is* Hirschman's mechanism: loyalty built through the
relationships raises the cost of exit, and the rising cost of exit is what converts the
energy into voice. The draft has independently arrived at the book's central dynamic, and
saying so makes the ending an argument rather than a mood.

**The correction.** "Neglect" is not Hirschman's. The four-way exit–voice–loyalty–neglect
typology is Farrell (1983) and Rusbult, Farrell, Rogers & Mainous (1988), who added neglect
as a distinct passive-destructive response. The draft's sentence — most default to neglect,
now called quiet quitting — attributes to Hirschman a category he did not propose. Given
that this project's defining constraint is tracing each claim to the person who first stated
it, this is the one citation error in the new material that is worth fixing before the
bibliography is updated.

### 33. Promotion is a lagging indicator of a story someone else has been telling about you

**Goffman (1959)** — *transfer* · *situation*

Goffman supplies the mechanism: the self an audience responds to is constructed from the
performance they have witnessed, which means the story precedes the recognition. He does not
supply the career claim — that promotion specifically lags narrative is Pfeffer [33] and
Ibarra [33], both already tagged in the further reading. On stage the honest form is
"Goffman explains why; Pfeffer shows it in careers."

**Peter & Hull (1969)** — *analogy*, and it is satire

**The source.** *The Peter Principle* is a comic book with a serious argument: in a
hierarchy, every employee tends to rise to their level of incompetence. Its vocabulary is
the useful part — *percussive sublimation* for being kicked upstairs, *lateral arabesque*
for being moved aside with a longer title, *final placement* for arriving at the level you
cannot handle.

**How the beat applies it.** As the name for the org's promotion bug, which the beat then
proposes to run forward deliberately. The vocabulary earns its place because it describes
outcomes an audience has watched happen and had no words for.

**The honesty requirement.** Peter and Hull wrote satire, not research, and a talk that
leans on the principle should say so in the same breath as it cites Benson, Li & Shue for
the evidence. The division of labour is clean — Peter and Hull named the pattern, the 2019
study measured it — but presenting the satire as a finding is the kind of thing a sharp
audience catches, and the outline's own citation discipline already forbids it.

**Where it strains, and it is worth tracking.** The measured evidence comes from sales
organisations, where current-role performance is unusually easy to quantify. That is the
setting most favourable to the Peter Principle's mechanism, so the generalisation to all
hierarchies is the author's rather than the study's. The working draft's Q&A answer about
the blast radius is the right instinct; the scope caveat belongs next to it.

## Act VI: Self-Defense

### 34. The same techniques that make you effective make you a target

**Mitnick & Simon (2002)** — *direct* · *polarity* · *build*

**The source.** *The Art of Deception* is built from pretexting case studies, and the thread
through all of them is that the exploited behaviour is a *virtue*: helpfulness, deference to
apparent authority, the wish not to look obstructive, the habit of answering a reasonable-sounding
question. Mitnick's prescriptive half, in the book's later part, is deliberately procedural
rather than attitudinal — verification policies, callbacks to numbers from the company
directory, defined authorisation steps — because he argues you cannot reliably train
suspicion into people, so you give them an authorised, blame-free way to verify.

**How the beat applies it.** As the hinge of the talk, and it can be sharpened. The beat
currently says the techniques that make you effective make you a target. The source supports
something more uncomfortable and more interesting: the *specific* virtues the first five
acts just taught — rapport, generosity with help, making work legible, being the person
people route through — are the attack surface Mitnick's case studies exploit. Act VI is not
a change of subject. It is the bill for Act IV.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Mitnick carries four beats — 34, 36, 39, 47 — and the
procedural-not-attitudinal principle is what unifies them. Every defence in this act is a
ritual rather than a state of alertness, and that is a choice the source argues for.
The D&D framing in the working draft lands on the same point: an Insight check is a roll you
make, not a mood you maintain. Verizon's breach report [34] is the modern incidence data.

### 35. Urgency, authority, and isolation are the three-ingredient recipe for every manipulation you'll face

**Cialdini (1984)** · **Milgram (1974)** · **Asch (1951)** — *synthesis*, from three *transfers*

**What each one actually supplies.** Cialdini supplies scarcity, which becomes urgency, and
authority as a compliance principle. Milgram supplies the depth of the authority effect and,
more usefully, its *conditions*: obedience fell sharply when the experimenter was absent and
gave instructions by telephone, and some participants then quietly administered lower shocks
than instructed. Asch supplies isolation — the pressure requires unanimity, and a single
other voice dissolves most of it.

**The honest framing.** No source states this recipe. It is the author's synthesis, and the
three citations support the three ingredients separately. That is a legitimate construction
and it should be presented as one, because a security-literate audience will know that
"urgency, authority, isolation" is a practitioner's heuristic rather than a finding. The
best-evidenced published catalogue of these ingredients is Stajano & Wilson's seven
principles [35], already in the further reading, which includes time pressure, social
compliance and distraction.

**The structural find.** The three ingredients have three counter-moves, and the outline
already contains all three, scattered: "I'll get back to you" at 39 defeats urgency, the
out-of-band check at 47 defeats authority, and "who else is involved?" at 25 and here
defeats isolation. What is worth knowing is that *two of the three counter-moves are the
experimental conditions under which the original effects collapsed*. Milgram's obedience
fell when the authority was at a distance; Asch's conformity fell when unanimity broke.
The defences are not clever improvisations against the studies — they are the studies'
own control conditions, run on purpose. That is the single strongest thing that can be said
for this act, and it is available from citations the outline already has.

### 36. Phishing and bad-faith persuasion share a threat model

**Cialdini (1984)** · **Mitnick & Simon (2002)** — *direct* · *polarity*

**Division of labour.** Cialdini supplies the principles being exploited; Mitnick supplies
the kill chain — research the target, establish rapport, exploit the trust, exit cleanly —
which is the part that makes "threat model" more than a metaphor. The academic mapping of
Cialdini's principles onto phishing is Ferreira, Coventry & Lenzini [36], already in the
further reading, and the notes correctly identify it as the available replacement if the
Hadnagy citations are dropped.

**What the beat's working-draft content rests on.** The draft's argument here — the first
step in a threat model is working out what the attacker wants; an ego-driven manager wants
standing, not results; so bad faith is instrumental and lands on whoever threatens standing;
so become the source of their standing and you stop being a target — is reasoning, not
citation, and it is the most consequential uncited passage in the outline. It is also
defensible from sources the outline already holds: Gouldner's second demand at 21 makes
injuring a benefactor the norm's strongest prohibition, and Miller & Ross at 25 supports the
credit-taking half. Attaching those two would move this from a plausible claim to a
supported one without changing what the beat says.

**The stage discipline the draft already specifies** — describe the behaviour, never
diagnose the person; "ego-driven," never a clinical term — is beat 24's thesis applied under
pressure, and it is the same rule as treating Dunning–Kruger as a framework rather than a
diagnosis.

### 37. Weaponized vagueness: how ambiguity is used to move accountability onto you

**Eisenberg (1984)** — *direct* on one function of several · *elicit*

**The source.** "Ambiguity as Strategy in Organizational Communication" coined *strategic
ambiguity*, and its argument is that clarity is not a universal good. Ambiguity performs
work: it promotes "unified diversity," letting people with different readings cooperate
around a shared symbol; it facilitates change by leaving room to reinterpret; it preserves
privileged positions; and it enables deniability. Eisenberg's account is functional, not
accusatory.

**How the beat applies it.** The beat takes two of the four functions — preserving position
and deniability — which is a fair reading, and the working draft is careful to add that a
struggling manager may not be doing it on purpose while the effect on you is identical.

**The refinement the source supplies, and the outline needs.** Because Eisenberg's account
is functional, it also distinguishes the kinds of vagueness, and that distinction is exactly
what the practice is missing. Not all ambiguity should be dissolved. Some of it is buying
adaptability in work that genuinely cannot be specified yet — which is Locke's
task-complexity moderator at beat 16 arriving from the other direction. The usable test is in
Eisenberg's own terms: *whose interest does this ambiguity serve, and is the deniability
one-way?* Ambiguity that leaves both parties room to adapt is doing Eisenberg's first job.
Ambiguity that leaves only one party room to deny is doing his third. The first should be
converted into a learning goal; the second should be converted into a written decision.

**Why this matters beyond the beat.** This is the answer to the strongest objection the talk
will face — that a team asking "what does done look like?" about everything is a team that
has mistaken precision for progress. The objection is correct about some work, the citation
already in the outline says so, and having the criterion ready turns a Q&A ambush into a
demonstration of the same judgment the talk is selling.

### 38. The verbal commitment with no written trace is a durability problem, not a trust problem

**Bartlett (1932)** — *transfer* · *record*

**How the beat applies it.** This is the outline's best piece of rhetorical engineering, and
the citation is what makes it work. Because Bartlett showed that *honest* people
systematically misremember — assimilating material to their own schema, shortening and
rationalising it — the recap can be presented as a defence against a cognitive fact rather
than an accusation of bad faith. "Drop the blame, keep the facts" is not a de-escalation
tactic bolted on afterwards; it is what the evidence actually licenses.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** With beat 9 this is the full *record* argument: information
degrades directionally in transit, so a written recap is the only artifact that does not,
and because degradation is universal the artifact implies nothing about anyone's character.
Loftus [38] extends it to suggestibility, which is the stronger and more adversarial version.

**The cost to price in, from beat 22.** Scott's critique predicts that what you make legible
becomes governable by people who lack your practical knowledge. The recap is a parachute and
a simplified model of your work, and the outline currently claims the first without pricing
the second. Keeping recaps to decisions, dates and tradeoffs — rather than to metrics that
can be adopted as targets — is the mitigation, and it is worth saying while the beat is
making the parachute claim.

### 39. "I'll get back to you" is a complete sentence and a security control

**Mitnick & Simon (2002)** — *direct* · *polarity* · *elicit*

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and the key is Mitnick's insistence that verification
must be *policy*. The beat's real content is not that the pause is wise — most people
already suspect that — but that it has to be socially cheap, which only happens when it is
normal. A pause that requires courage will not be taken at the moment it is most needed.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is where Act VI's three strands converge. Asch at 23
and 35 measures the cost of being the only person who hesitates; Milgram's telephone
condition at 35 shows that distance from the authority is what restores judgment; Mitnick
prescribes the policy that creates the distance on demand. In the working draft the same
sentence also does duty inside the practice, as the gap between the ask and the recap, which
makes it the clearest case in the outline of a single move that is simultaneously an
influence technique and a security control — *polarity* in one sentence. Ury [39] is the
negotiation version.

### 40. Spotting the difference between a request and a quota being transferred to you

**Oncken & Wass (1974)** — *inversion*

**The source.** The monkey is the next move in a piece of work. The article's thesis is that
managers drown because they accept monkeys from their subordinates, and its prescriptions
are an accounting discipline: every monkey must be described, assigned to a named owner,
given a risk level, and given a scheduled feeding time. The audience is managers, and the
direction of concern is upward-to-downward.

**How the beat applies it.** Pointed the other way. The beat uses the same accounting to
refuse an unowned next move being handed *down*. That inversion is clean, and it is the
reason the citation survives having no empirical base at all — what transfers is not a
finding but a discipline for naming whose move it is.

**The mechanism, stated precisely.** The priority question — if I can only do two of these
three, which two? — does not clarify the work. It relocates the monkey. Vague expectations
are fixed by clarifying; impossible ones can only be fixed by making the owner choose, and
once the ranking is in writing the tradeoff has a named owner, which is Oncken and Wass's
first rule.

**Where it strains.** The notes already call this the thinnest beat in the outline: an *HBR*
essay from 1974 with no study behind it, and a metaphor that has aged. The compensating
strength is that the beat's content is a procedure rather than a claim about the world, so
it does not need evidence in the way beat 15 does. The honest stage framing is "a discipline
borrowed from management writing," not "research shows."

### 41. Burnout is often a social-engineering outcome, not a personal failing

**Freudenberger (1974)** — *origin, not the evidence* · *situation*

**The source.** Freudenberger coined the clinical sense of burnout from observing staff at
free clinics, and described the syndrome — exhaustion, cynicism, rigidity, suspicion,
over-identification with the work. His account of the cause is partly *dispositional*: the
most committed and dedicated people are the most at risk, because they over-invest.

**The problem this creates for the beat.** The beat's claim is that burnout is a structural
outcome rather than a personal failing. Freudenberger is the origin of the term, but his
mechanism points at the person's own dedication, which is closer to the view the beat is
arguing against. This is the same origin-versus-evidence split the notes already flag at
beats 3, 42, 48 and 52 — except here it is not flagged, and the mismatch runs in the awkward
direction.

**What the beat's claim actually rests on.** Two citations, one of which the working draft
has already added. **Karasek (1979)** is the precise one: strain is produced by the
*interaction* of high job demands with low decision latitude, and a high-demand job with
high control is not a strained job but an "active" one. That makes the practice's
contribution specific and measurable in principle — elicitation adds decision latitude
without reducing demands, which is a move from one Karasek quadrant to another. The second
is already in the further reading: Maslach & Leiter on the job-context model, and the WHO's
2019 classification of burn-out as an occupational phenomenon [41], which is the single
strongest support anywhere for "not a personal failing."

**Hochschild (1983)** — *direct*, with a terminological note

*The Managed Heart* names emotional labour and distinguishes surface acting from deep
acting. One precision, since this project cares about it: Hochschild's *emotional labour* is
specifically feeling managed for a wage under an employer's feeling rules, while unpaid
emotion management in private life is her *emotion work*. The draft's "unpaid emotional
labour" is, in her terms, emotion work — common usage has drifted, and a sociology-literate
audience member may say so.

**The link worth keeping.** Hochschild's surface/deep acting distinction maps onto the
ethical floor better than anything else in the outline. Elicitation performed as surface
acting — the generous read displayed but not believed — is ingratiation, the move beat 51
rejects. Performed as deep acting, it is beat 24's generous read actually held. The
difference is invisible from outside and decisive from inside, which is why the floor has to
be a rule the practitioner applies to themselves.

## Act VII: The AI Inflection

### 42. AI collapsed the cost of producing competent-looking output to approximately zero

**Simon (1971)** — *direct*, and it does more work here than at 13 · *scarcity* · *mirror*

Simon's design criterion is the part of the citation this beat needs: a useful
information-processing system *absorbs* demands on attention rather than generating more
information. By that criterion a tool that produces more polished artifacts faster is not an
attention-saving technology at all — it is an attention-consuming one wearing the costume of
the other thing. That is a precise, citable reason why beat 43 is a *consequence* of beat 42
rather than the next slide, and it comes from a source the outline already cites at both.

**Noy & Zhang (2023)** — *direct*

**The study.** 453 college-educated professionals performing mid-level professional writing
tasks — press releases, short reports, analysis plans. Access to ChatGPT cut time on task by
about 40 per cent and raised graders' quality ratings by about 18 per cent. The third result
is the one this outline should care about most: inequality in output quality *fell*. The tool
helped the weakest writers most, compressing the distribution.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct on the cost claim. The compression result is the deeper
support and it is currently unused.

**The link that is already in the data.** If output quality converges across skill levels,
then output quality stops carrying information about the person who produced it. That is not
a rhetorical flourish about AI — it is what the experiment measured, and it is exactly the
heuristic beat 44 says broke. "Polished means competent" did not fail because of deepfakes
first; it failed because the correlation it depended on was measured away in a writing
experiment. Beat 42 and beat 44 are therefore one argument with evidence in the middle, and
the outline currently presents them as two observations in sequence.

### 43. When everyone can generate the artifact, judgment and trust become the scarce goods

**Simon (1971)** · **Arrow (1972)** — *direct* · *scarcity* · *mirror*

**Division of labour, and it is the cleanest in the outline.** Simon explains why attention
becomes the binding constraint when artifacts are abundant. Arrow explains why trust cannot
simply be produced to meet the new demand: it is not a commodity that can be bought, which
is precisely what makes it scarce in the economic sense rather than merely valuable. One
citation supplies the scarcity, the other supplies the non-substitutability. Together they
make this the most rigorous beat in Act VII, and neither does the job alone.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** This is the beat that earns the talk its subject. If
judgment and trust are the scarce goods, then the skills in Acts III–VI are not soft
complements to engineering, they are the constrained resource. Beat 48 is the empirical
version of the same claim, and beat 52 is its one-sentence form.

### 44. Deepfakes and synthetic pretexting broke the heuristics your instincts were trained on

**Turing (1950)** — *analogy*, and the right one

**The source.** Turing's move was to replace the unanswerable question — can machines
think — with an operational one: can a machine, communicating *through a teleprinter*, be
distinguished from a human by an interrogator. The channel restriction is not incidental to
the imitation game. It is the design. Turing deliberately stripped out voice, face and body
so that the test would be about the conversation and nothing else.

**How the beat applies it, and the sharper reading available.** The usual gloss is "machines
passing as human." The more useful one for this outline is that Turing identified *the
channel as the thing that determines what can be faked*. He chose text because text was the
channel where imitation was conceivable in 1950. Deepfakes did not invent a new problem;
they extended the imitation game into the channels where human instincts were strongest and
therefore least examined — the voice on the phone, the face on the video call.

**Why that reading matters three beats later.** It makes out-of-band verification at 47 a
principle rather than a tip. The defence against imitation is to move the exchange to a
channel the imitator has not mastered and does not control. "Out-of-band" is "outside the
imitation game," which is a better sentence than the security-training version and it comes
free with a citation the outline already has.

*The working draft's case and companions.* The Arup deepfake video call, where a finance
worker's initial suspicion of a "secret transaction" was dissolved by a call full of
synthetic colleagues and roughly 25 million US dollars moved, is reported by Chen & Magramo
[44]. Nightingale & Farid [44] supply the controlled finding that synthetic faces are not
merely indistinguishable from real ones but are rated *more* trustworthy — which is the
strongest possible support for the beat's claim that the instinct is not just unreliable but
inverted. The draft's reading of the case is the sharp part: a workforce trained never to
question the boss is the attack surface, so the questions habit is the control. That claim
belongs to this project and nothing in the further reading says it.

### 45. The AI-assisted engineer's new job title is "person who vouches for this"

**Bainbridge (1983)** — *transfer* · *situation* · *mirror*

**The source.** "Ironies of Automation" is four arguments, and all four transfer. The
designer automates the parts that are easy to automate, which leaves the operator the
residue — the hard parts. Manual skill decays when the task is not practised, so the
operator becomes less able to take over the longer the automation works. Humans are poor at
monitoring for rare events, which is exactly what oversight asks of them. And takeover is
required precisely in the situations the automation could not handle, which are the worst
ones.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct transfer to AI-assisted engineering, and the second
irony is the one that supports the beat's title: vouching for output you no longer routinely
produce yourself is a skill that erodes while you rely on it.

**The link to Act VI.** Bainbridge's third irony — humans monitor rare events badly — is the
same cognitive limit Mitnick builds his prescriptions around at 34 and 47. It is why both
acts answer with *rituals* rather than vigilance. The outline arrives at procedural defences
twice, from automation research and from security practice, and saying that out loud makes
the recommendation look like convergence rather than preference.

**Peter & Hull (1969)**, in the working draft, as the *mirror* move: AI promoted every
engineer to manager of its output with no training, which is the Peter Principle applied to
the audience in the room rather than to their boss. That is the empathy mechanism the earlier
acts need, and placing it here — after Act V has spent seven beats on a manager's failures —
is the structurally smartest thing in the revised outline. Parasuraman & Manzey [45] carry
the complacency evidence and Dell'Acqua et al. [45] the jagged-frontier result.

### 46. AI is a persuasion amplifier: it drafts, tailors, and A/B tests the message against you

**Weizenbaum (1976)** — *direct* · *disclose*

**The source.** *Computer Power and Human Reason* is the ELIZA author's warning against his
own program's reception. The detail that matters: people attributed understanding to ELIZA,
confided in it, and asked to be left alone with it *while knowing it was a program*.
Weizenbaum's broader argument separates deciding, which a machine can do, from choosing,
which he argued requires judgment a machine cannot have.

**How the beat applies it.** As the origin of the claim that people are readily persuaded by
machines. The precise finding is stronger than that and it cuts both ways.

**The complication worth facing, because it is the best objection to beat 51.** The ELIZA
effect survives disclosure. Weizenbaum's subjects knew, and it worked anyway. That is a
documented case where knowing does *not* protect the person being influenced, which means the
disclosure test cannot be defended as a guarantee of the target's safety. It has to be
defended as what it actually is: a constraint the practitioner accepts on themselves, a
negative criterion in Kant's sense. The outline's floor survives this, but only in the
modest form. Stated in the strong form — if they know, they are safe — Weizenbaum is the
counterexample, and a sharp audience member has it available.

**The positive use the working draft makes.** Rehearsing against an AI that plays your vague
manager works *because* the ELIZA effect works: the simulation is persuasive enough to train
on. The draft's sparring-partner move depends on the same phenomenon Weizenbaum warned
about, which is worth saying because it is honest and because it is a good line.

**Cialdini (1984)** supplies what gets amplified — the six principles are the content the
tailoring optimises. Hazell [46] on LLM spear phishing and Salvi et al. [46] on
conversational persuasiveness in a randomised trial are the modern measurements, both
already in the further reading.

### 47. Verification rituals must now be out-of-band by default

**Mitnick & Simon (2002)** — *direct* · *polarity* · *elicit*

The prescription is literal: call back on a number you obtained independently, not one you
were given. The beat is the source's own recommendation restated for a world where the
pretext arrives with a face.

**The two things this beat unifies.** First, the working draft's observation that
verification with an AI *is* the recap — ask it to restate the task before it starts — makes
this beat beat 17 run in reverse, and the symmetry is real: in both directions the party
who will do the work certifies their understanding to the party who asked. Second, Turing
at 44 supplies the principle under "out-of-band": leave the channel the imitator controls.
The outline has the tactic and the principle three beats apart and does not connect them.

**The NSA/FBI/CISA sheet** [47] is the institutional backing, already in the further
reading, and it is the right citation to show a security audience that the recommendation is
not idiosyncratic.

### 48. The skills AI cannot commoditize are the ones this talk is about

**Deming (2017)** — *direct*, and it is the project's load-bearing empirical claim

**The study.** Between 1980 and 2012, social-skill-intensive occupations grew by close to
twelve percentage points as a share of the US labour force, while math-intensive but less
social occupations — many STEM roles among them — shrank by 3.3 points. The interaction is
the headline result: occupations requiring *both* high math and high social skills grew 7.2
points and saw roughly 26 per cent wage growth over the period. Deming's model explains why.
Social skills lower the cost of coordinating and trading tasks, which is what makes team
production worth more than the sum of individual work.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and the mechanism is the part to say on stage, because
it is what makes the claim about AI rather than about niceness. If social skills reduce the
cost of trading tasks, then anything that multiplies the number of task-trades raises their
value. AI multiplies task-trades — with the model, and between people who now review each
other's generated work. Coordination cost per unit of output becomes the binding constraint,
which is Brooks at 4 and Simon at 13 arriving as a labour-market result.

**What it holds up elsewhere, including a beat it is not cited at.** The interaction result
is the exact structure of beat 52's close: not charisma instead of competence, but the two
together, with the measured premium on the combination. Deming supports beat 52 at least as
well as Casciaro & Lobo does, and in a form — thirty-two years of labour-market data — that
is harder to argue with than an *HBR* network study. The outline cites him only at 48.

## Act VIII: The Build

### 49. Your charisma build, speced: three stats to raise and one to respec out of

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book II.1** — *direct* · *build*

II.1 names the three components of ethos — good sense, good moral character, goodwill — and
the notes have already confirmed this against the text. The three stats are therefore not an
analogy; they are a list, in the source, in the right order of operations for a speaker.

**Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti (2011)** — *direct* · *build*

**Division of labour.** Aristotle supplies what to raise; Antonakis supplies the drills and
the evidence that drilling works. The twelve charismatic leadership tactics are the trainable
layer under Aristotle's three components, which is what makes "speced" more than a joke: a
stat you can only admire is a trait, and a stat with twelve named exercises under it is a
build.

**A modern mapping already in the further reading.** Fiske, Cuddy & Glick's warmth and
competence [49] are, within a reasonable margin, Aristotle's goodwill and practical wisdom
arriving as the two universal dimensions of social cognition. That is a strong independent
convergence on a 2,400-year-old list and it is sitting unused at this beat.

**Where the metaphor seams show.** The notes already observe that the gaming vocabulary
appears in beats 3 and 49 and the title, and nowhere else. There is a related seam inside
this beat: the three stats come from Aristotle, but the fourth item — Deception, the one to
respec out of — comes from D&D's skill list, not from Aristotle, who has no fourth component
of ethos to subtract. The beat is doing something slightly different from what it says: it
raises three things from one framework and declines a fourth from another. That is fine on
stage and worth knowing in the book, where the two frameworks will have to be reconciled or
one of them dropped.

### 50. Week one: the smallest experiments that produce visible returns

**Weick (1984)** — *direct*

**The source.** "Small Wins" argues that the *scale at which a problem is defined* is itself
a cause of failure: framing something as massive produces arousal and helplessness that
impair the very performance needed to address it. A small win is a concrete, complete,
implemented outcome of moderate importance. Weick's claims about them are specific — they
lower arousal to a workable level, they attract allies, they reveal resources that were
invisible before, and although their order is largely unpredictable in advance, they
accumulate into a pattern that can be recognised afterwards.

**How the beat applies it.** Direct, and the fit is better than "start small" suggests. The
problem this talk's audience walks in holding — my manager cannot define done, the
organisation promotes the wrong people, the deal at work is broken — is a textbook
massively-scaled problem of exactly the kind Weick says produces paralysis. One question on
Monday and one recap is a small win in his technical sense: concrete, complete, visible, and
of moderate importance.

**What it holds up elsewhere.** Weick's second property — small wins attract allies — is the
mechanism behind the working draft's water-cooler spread at beat 25, where one person asking
the question becomes a team norm. The outline currently cites Weick only here, while the
diffusion argument happens there. Amabile & Kramer [50] and Fogg [50] carry the modern
versions.

### 51. The ethical floor: if it only works when they don't know you're doing it, don't do it

**Kant (1795), Appendix II** — *direct* · *disclose*

The negative formula is the floor's foundation, and the *affirmative* formula in the same
appendix — maxims that require publicity in order not to fail of their end are in accordance
with both right and politics — is the one that actually fits this project's signature
technique, because elicitation's success condition is being discovered. See beat 5. Keeping
the floor in its negative form is defensible and modest; adding the affirmative form is what
lets the beat claim that the practice is not merely permitted but positively vindicated.

**Plato, *Meno*** — *direct* on the method's risk, and the source of the best structural
find in the outline

**The source.** Socrates teaches entirely by question, including the famous demonstration
with Meno's slave, who is led through a geometrical construction and appears to recover the
answer himself. The criticism the working draft cites is the right one and it is
long-standing: Socrates supplies the diagram, the sequence and the moment of agreement, so
the questions are not neutral. The dialogue is the strongest available illustration of both
halves of the beat — teaching by question, and leading by question, performed by the same
person in the same conversation.

**The find.** *Meno*'s actual subject is whether virtue can be taught. That is beat 3's
question — whether charisma can be taught — asked about a different excellence twenty-three
centuries earlier. And the dialogue's provisional answer is that virtue appears to be not
knowledge but true opinion, arriving by something like divine dispensation, which is
functionally Weber's position on charisma: an extraordinary gift, not a curriculum. So the
outline's first act opens with Weber's "it cannot be taught" and its last act closes with the
dialogue that reached the same conclusion first, while the beat that answers both — Antonakis's
controlled training experiment — sits at 3 and 49. That is a closed loop across the whole
talk, built entirely from citations the outline already has, and it is available as an
epigraph, a cold open, or a callback in the final minute.

**Two supports for the floor that the outline has not claimed.** First, from beat 20's new
material: Kipnis & Schmidt found that reason-led tacticians received the highest performance
ratings while ingratiators landed in the middle. The outline rejects ingratiation on ethical
grounds; the measurement says it also underperforms. Ethics and effectiveness point the same
way, with evidence, which is a much stronger place to stand than ethics alone. Second, from
beat 18: face-work is a technique everyone knows about that works anyway, which is the
existence proof the floor needs against the objection that disclosure kills all influence.
And one limit, from beat 46: the ELIZA effect survives disclosure, so the floor must be
stated as a constraint on the practitioner, not as a guarantee for the target.

### 52. Leave with this: competence gets you in the room, charisma determines whether the room does anything about it

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric*, Book I.2** — *direct*

I.2's claim that the speaker's character is close to the most effective of the proofs is the
origin of the beat's second clause, and citing the *Rhetoric* at the close after citing it at
19, 30 and 49 lets the talk end where it started without announcing that it is doing so.

**Casciaro & Lobo (2005)** — *direct*

**The study.** Roughly ten thousand work relationships across five organisations, sorted into
four archetypes: the competent jerk, the lovable fool, the lovable star and the incompetent
jerk. The finding: when choosing whom to work with, people overwhelmingly prefer the lovable
fool to the competent jerk, whatever they say about valuing competence. The lovable star is
sought by everyone.

**How the beat applies it.** As modern evidence for Aristotle's ranking, and the fit is good
because their dependent variable is *whom people choose to work with* — which is access, and
access is precisely what the beat's second clause is about. Competence gets you in the room;
whether anyone routes work, trust or a decision through you is a partner-choice question, and
that is the question they measured.

**Two things to handle on stage.** First, the beat's recommendation is the lovable *star*,
not the lovable fool — the study is often quoted as though it endorsed charm over ability,
and the talk's whole argument is the conjunction. Deming's interaction result at 48 is the
better-evidenced form of exactly that conjunction, and it belongs in the close. Second, the
same paper reports the cost: people who like each other tend to share values and ways of
thinking, so networks built on likability homogenise. A talk recommending likability as a
career strategy should be able to say that out loud, especially given the composition
concern the notes already raise about the origin list. It is also a Q&A question waiting to
be asked.

## Source spines

Fifteen sources are cited on more than one beat. Read beat by beat, each is a supporting
citation. Read end to end, each is an argument that crosses acts — which is the clearest
answer to how disjointed the outline really is. The spines are the structure already
present in it.

**Aristotle, *Rhetoric* — 19, 30, 49, 52.** The talk's rhetoric layer, and the only source
that appears in four separate acts. The progression is coherent and almost certainly
unplanned: proofs exist (19), address them to this audience (30), these are the three to
train (49), and character is the strongest of them (52). If the talk needs one classical
spine on a slide, this is it, and the four beats are already in the right order.

**Cialdini — 2, 14, 35, 36, 46.** The *polarity* spine, and the most load-bearing single
reading in the outline. The same six principles are provenance (2), toolkit (14), attack
recipe (35), shared threat model (36) and the thing AI amplifies (46). Worth knowing that
the book's own framing is defensive, so the Act VI uses are faithful and the Act III use is
the one that departs.

**Mitnick & Simon — 34, 36, 39, 47.** The self-defence spine, unified by a principle the
outline does not state: defences must be procedural, not attitudinal, because suspicion
cannot be reliably trained. Every one of these four beats is a ritual rather than a state of
mind, and that is an argument the source makes explicitly.

**Barnard — 7, 20, 27, plus the core belief.** The project's foundation. Informal graph (7),
acceptance pointed laterally (20), the four conditions as the question set (27). The unused
piece — providing the system of communication as the first executive function — would make
the recap practice a Barnard move rather than an improvisation.

**Asch — 23, 25, 35.** The social-pressure spine: the cost of dissenting (23), the ally that
removes it (25), isolation as an attack ingredient (35). One experiment supplies a cost, a
remedy and a threat, which is unusually efficient.

**Goffman — 18, 28, 33, plus face-work in the revised draft.** The performance spine, and
the source doing the most unacknowledged work in the outline. Status cannot be abstained
from (18), you and your manager are a team before their boss (28), the audience builds the
story that promotion lags (33). Face-work also supplies the ethical floor's existence proof.

**Simon — 13, 42, 43.** The attention spine, and it tightens considerably once the design
half of the 1971 paper is included: attention is scarce (13), AI generates more claims on it
(42), so what stays scarce is what cannot be generated (43). Three beats, one argument, two
acts apart.

**Milgram — 25, 35 in the revised draft.** Cited twice in opposite directions: as the
measure of how far authority reaches (35) and as the measure of how little it takes to break
it (25). The second is the better use and the less familiar finding.

**Peter & Hull — 4, 33, 45 in the revised draft.** The newest spine and structurally the
cleverest: the difficulty modifier (4), the mechanism the balloon exploits (33), and then
the same principle turned on the audience, who have just been promoted to manager of an
AI's output (45). It is the arc that converts complaint into empathy.

**Bartlett — 9, 38.** *record*, both halves: why the recap is necessary, and why it is not an
accusation.

**Shannon — 12, 28.** The two analogies, both about loss rather than meaning, which is the
only Shannon application a technical audience will accept.

**Weber — 3, 18, and uncited in Act VI's premise.** Charisma as unteachable gift (3), status
as an independent variable (18), legal-rational authority as the thing that both grants and
bounds a manager's power (the revised Act VI preamble). The third use deserves the citation
it is already relying on.

**Arrow — 10, 43.** Trust as lubricant (10) and as the good that cannot be manufactured to
meet new demand (43). The second use is what makes Act VII an economic argument.

**Kram — 26, 32.** Mentoring pointed upward (26) and sponsorship distinguished from
affection (32). The four phases, unused, are the grounding for the end date.

**Rogers & Roethlisberger — 17, 23.** The restatement rule as an opening move (17) and as a
protocol for disagreement (23). The "to that speaker's satisfaction" clause is an acceptance
test, which makes this spine a sibling of Barnard's.

**Kant — 5, 51.** The deliberate bookend, and the one structure the notes say will not
survive the book. Both beats currently use the negative formula; the affirmative formula
sits in the same appendix.

**Antonakis, Fenley & Liechti — 3, 49.** The evidence that the title is not a metaphor:
charisma trained (3), and the twelve tactics as the drill list under Aristotle's three
components (49).

### Five worth acting on first

1. **Kant's affirmative formula** sits in the same appendix as the one already cited, and it
   fits elicitation better than the negative formula does, because the practice's success

2. **Meno's subject is whether an excellence can be taught**, and the dialogue's answer is

3. **Barnard's first executive function is providing the system of communication.** That
   makes the recap an executive function performed from below rather than a clever habit,
   and it explains *why* authority follows it. Beats 7, 9, 22, 28, 38.
4. **Asch's ally effect works by breaking unanimity, not by adding agreement.** The

   easier ask and it is what the experiment supports.
5. **Noy & Zhang found that output quality converged across skill levels.** That is the

   beats 42 and 44 are one argument with evidence in the middle.

### Where a beat and its citation have drifted apart

- **Beat 15** is the worst case, and worse than the notes record. The comparative claim —
  framing beats evidence — is not Tversky & Kahneman's; their result is that framing changes
  the answer while the evidence is held constant. Separately, the working draft's example for
  this beat ("so I don't waste your time" versus "you never told me") is face-work, which is
  Goffman at 18, not gain/loss framing. The citation and the illustration are pointing at
  different literatures.
- **Beat 6**'s operative content in the working draft is the blameless postmortem, which is
  Allspaw's and belongs to beat 23's citation. Wiener covers the first half of the sentence
  only.
- **Beat 19**'s ranking of narrative over data is not in *Rhetoric* I.2; Aristotle treats the
  three proofs as suited to different occasions and privileges reasoned proof. The claim the
  beat needs is II.1's observation that judgments differ according to the hearer's emotional
  state.
- **Beat 11**'s reputation-as-service-registry is Burt's structural holes rather than
  Granovetter's weak ties. Granovetter supplies reach, which does support the strongest form
  of the beat: your reputation is queried across a bridge.
- **Beat 8**'s "runs in both directions" is safe in the diagnostic direction and belongs to
  *Team Topologies* in the interventionist one. Conway argued the constraint, not the manoeuvre.
- **Beat 22** reverses Scott's valence. He is critiquing legibility; the beat recommends it.
  Defensible from inside and voluntarily, but it should be said rather than assumed.
- **Beat 33**'s career claim is Pfeffer and Ibarra; Goffman supplies the mechanism that makes
  it plausible, not the finding.
- **Beat 41**'s citation points mildly against its claim. Freudenberger's mechanism is partly
  dispositional — the most dedicated are most at risk — while the beat argues burnout is
  structural. Karasek, Maslach & Leiter, and the WHO classification carry the beat's actual
  claim; Freudenberger carries the word.

- **"Where each one fails at scale," in beat 14's own title.** Cialdini cannot support it;
  his principles are dyadic. Brooks at 4 and Simon at 13 are the available support, and this
  is the outline's largest uncited claim.
- **The threat-model reasoning in the working draft's beat 36** — that an ego-driven

- **"The meeting is a ratification ceremony," beat 25.** Asch supports the dissenter's

- **"A workforce trained never to question the boss is the attack surface for a deepfake,"

### Attributions to fix before the bibliography is updated

- **Neglect is not Hirschman's.** The four-way exit–voice–loyalty–neglect typology is Farrell
  (1983) and Rusbult, Farrell, Rogers & Mainous (1988). The working draft's beat 32 attributes
  it to *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. Hirschman's own third term is loyalty, and his mechanism —
  loyalty raises the cost of exit and so activates voice — is precisely what the draft's close
  describes, so the beat gains an argument by making the correction.
- **Benson, Li & Shue firm count.** The published study covers 214 firms; the earlier working
  paper covered 131 firms, 38,843 sales workers and 1,553 promotions, 2005–2011. The draft's
  "214 firms" should name the published version so the two numbers do not look like an error.
- **Hochschild's terms.** Emotional labour is waged and employer-governed; unpaid emotion
  management is *emotion work*. The draft's "unpaid emotional labour" is the latter.
- **Peter & Hull is satire.** Keep it for the vocabulary, pair it with Benson, Li & Shue for
  the evidence, and say which is which on stage.
- **Popper did not coin "social engineering."** The bibliography's "neutral use" is the right
  claim and worth defending as written.
- **Benson et al.'s setting is sales organisations**, where current-role performance is
  unusually measurable — the most favourable possible setting for the Peter Principle's
  mechanism. The generalisation to all hierarchies is the author's.
